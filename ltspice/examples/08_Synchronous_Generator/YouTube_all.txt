Synchronous generator:


In the next three examples, I will demonstrate the different operating modes of a synchronous generator. These are 

isolated operation mode 
operation connected to a stable network 
operation with fixed speed 

Isolated operation mode of a synchronous generator:

General:

“Isolated mode” means that there is no voltage source in the grid to maintain a constant voltage. In an isolated grid, it is therefore absolutely necessary to use an AVR (automatic voltage regulator) to maintain a stable voltage level. A speed controller is also required to meet load demands. We will also demonstrate how to connect a second synchronous generator to the isolated grid. The more generators feeding into the isolated grid, the more stable the system becomes.
 
Circuit design:
 
The schematic shows a synchronous generator rated at four hundred megawatts and twenty kVols with a round rotor, a transformer from twenty kilovolts to four hundred kVols, and a switch that connects the generator to the unloaded network at one seconds.

A load ramps from zero to fifty megawatts between two seconds and five seconds. An AVR, automatic voltage regulator, switches on at six seconds, and a speed controller, governor, is active for the entire simulation. The generator's main excitation field is supplied with a high voltage ramp during the first second, from six hundred volts to fourteen hundred volts and back to six hundred volts in order to quickly reach nominal voltage.

The AVR measures on the high voltage side with a target voltage of four hundred kilovolts. The speed controller has a set point of fifteen hundred RPM and remains active throughout the simulation. Its switch is set to five hundred seconds. All external controllers are implemented as simple PI controllers to illustrate the basic behavior of a controlled synchronous machine.

Their dynamics are intentionally fast to reduce simulation time. Notes on the AVR switching logic. The AVR switch must close shortly before the fixed field voltage switch opens. The magnetic field voltage must never drop to zero because the terminal voltage of the machine would also collapse. Since the AVR supplies high currents, the switch's on resistance must be very low to prevent the field voltage from dropping.

The torque control switches are less critical. The electromagnetic torque may be zero for a short time because the turbine and generator have a large inertia defined by the machine's J parameter, so the speed cannot suddenly fall to zero. 

Simulation start: 

The machine starts from zero voltage. To quickly reach nominal voltage, the AVR is initially set to fourteen hundred volts. The stator copper resistance parameter Rcu is set to a relatively high value of zero point zero one to accelerate voltage buildup for faster simulation. 

Voltage behavior:

After one second, the nominal voltages of four hundred K volts network and twenty K volts generator are reached due to the initially high AVR setting. When the load ramps from zero to fifty megawatt between two seconds and five seconds, the current increases and the generator voltage drops slightly from four hundred K volts to about three hundred ninety-seven kilovolts. At six seconds, the AVR activates and quickly restores the bus voltage to four hundred K volts.
In isolated mode, a change in voltage directly affects the generator's active power output because the load power depends on voltage. In grid-connected mode, the voltage is more stable, so active power does not change in this way. 


Speed behavior: 

During the load ramp, the speed falls below fifteen hundred RPM. Once the final load value is reached, the speed returns to fifteen hundred RPM. When the AVR switches on, the frequency briefly decreases because the rising voltage increases the load current, which increases torque demand. After the voltage stabilizes, the speed also stabilizes at fifteen hundred RPM.

Load angle behavior: 

The load angle increases during the load ramp and then stabilizes at about twelve degrees. Load step of thirty megawatt. A thirty-megawatt load step is introduced by setting V fact equals three at two seconds and adding a switch at three seconds. The load angle changes significantly, and the speed shows transient behavior. Additional load steps at five seconds and seven seconds cause further changes in frequency and load angle. The generator output jumps from thirty megawatt to sixty megawatts to ninety megawatt with a maximum load angle of about twenty-one degrees. 

Salient pole machine comparison:

Setting Schenkel pole factor 0.5 and KC 0.8 models a salient pole machine. After restarting the simulation, the load angle is only about seven degrees compared to twenty-one degrees for the round rotor machine. 

Reactive power in isolated mode:

Unlike a grid-connected generator, an isolated generator cannot change reactive power output by adjusting field voltage. Both active and reactive power are determined solely by the load. The field voltage only influences the terminal voltage. If the load is a constant power load, the AVR has no influence on active power. 

Switching off loads:

Loads one, two, and three are switched off at nine seconds, eleven seconds, and thirteen seconds. The load angle decreases accordingly. After the final load is removed, the frequency takes a long time to settle back to fifty hertz because only friction torque slows the generator. Nominal frequency is reached after about three point five seconds. When load is removed, frequency overshoots above nominal, the opposite of what happens during positive load steps. 

Unit conversion for plots:

To display values in correct units, load angle in degrees multiply by one degree or one volt.
Frequency in hertz multiply by one hertz, one volt. Active power in watts multiply by one watt or one volt. 

Synchronizing a second generator:

A second generator is added on the right side, including its speed controller, but without an AVR. Simulation end time is set to thirty seconds. Load three switches at twenty seconds to show a load step after synchronization. Generator two is connected at twelve seconds. Its speed controller stops at twelve seconds, and constant torque begins at twelve point one seconds. A ramp from zero to twenty megawatts between fourteen seconds and eighteen seconds defines its active power output. 

Synchronization conditions:

At around nine point eight to ten point two seconds, the generator and network voltages have nearly the same magnitude, and generator two leads slightly in phase. To correct this, the speed controller set point can be adjusted to fifteen hundred RPM minus twenty millihertz, forty-nine point nine eight hertz. At twelve seconds, both voltages are in phase and synchronization is successful. 

Power sharing:

Generator two delivers twenty megawatts, so generator one reduces its output to forty megawatts. A subsequent thirty-megawatt load step is supplied entirely by generator one because generator two has no active speed controller. Final outputs, gen one delivers seventy megawatts, and gen two delivers twenty megawatts. Generator two shows larger frequency transients because there is no speed controller present.

Conclusion:

A synchronous generator operating in isolated mode requires voltage and speed control. The voltage in the island grid is highly dependent on the load. After the second generator has synchronized, the second generator must not have voltage or speed control. Active power is delivered here via an appropriate torque on the generator shaft.

Grid operation mode and constant speed operation:

General:

Now we will examine the synchronous generator in grid operation and in constant speed operation. We will simulate synchronization and the subsequent delivery of active and reactive power. We will also discuss short circuits near and far from the generator, particularly the single-pole ground fault. We will also demonstrate what happens when the maximum active power delivered by the machine is exceeded. 

Circuit design: 

The circuit contains from left to right, a synchronous generator with thirty-six hundred RPM at sixty hertz and a capacity of two hundred and fifty Megavar ampere, a speed governor, a small five megawatts load, an automatic voltage regulator on the thirteen and eight kilovolt side, a YD eleven transformer, a synchronization switch, a load rated at ten megawatts, a switch for the three-phase fault in the grid, a grid resistor and a two hundred and thirty kilovolt voltage source.

The transformer has a delta winding on the secondary side due to the high generator currents and to filter third harmonic currents. The primary side is connected in a star configuration due to the high voltages on the grid side The transformer is set to linear, parameter M equals one, to avoid nonlinear transients after the generator is switched on at full voltage, parameter air equals one.

The rated excitation voltage of the generator is two hundred volts. To maintain the rated voltage constantly at thirteen-point-eight kilovolts, an automatic voltage regulator, AVR, is provided, which is switched on at T equals point one seconds. The AVR has an excitation range from one hundred volts to four hundred volts to cover both overexcitation and underexcitation of the synchronous machine.

The speed controller has a rated speed of thirty-six hundred RPM and is active from the start. At the start of the simulation, the synchronous generator has a speed of three thousand six hundred RPM. If the synchronous generator is synchronized with the grid after one second, the speed controller is switched off immediately thereafter, and the synchronous generator is ramped up from zero megawatts to a hundred and fifty megawatts of active power between two and ten seconds.

The conversion from active power to the torque requirement for the synchronous generator is given by P equals N omega PM equals torque, P equals number of pole pairs. The number of pole pairs is one, making the generator a high-speed turbo generator. To synchronize the generator quickly starting at one second, the phase angle of the voltage source is set to three hundred and thirty degrees, DPHI equal three thirty.

This ensures rapid synchronization for a three-phase transformer with a rotating connection group of YD eleven, eleven, thirty degrees, three hundred and thirty degrees. Additionally, the AVR has already reached the rated voltage of thirteen point eight kilovolts at the terminals. 


Synchronization and active power output:

Now we start the simulation from zero to twelve seconds. You can see that the synchronization condition is well met at the one-second mark when looking at the voltage difference before and after the switch. In the top image, we see that the voltage difference is approximately four kilovolt. In the bottom image, we see that the frequency is very close to sixty hertz.

Synchronization causes transients in active power and frequency. The frequency remains above sixty hertz as long as the generator increases the active power at a rate of twenty-five megawatts per second. When the change in active power becomes zero, the frequency returns to sixty hertz. The active power ramp is achieved via a torque ramp at the MPIN torque pin of the generator 

Note: The active power ramp of twenty-five megawatts per second is of course much too high and serves here only to keep the simulation time short.

Three-pole generator short circuit to ground starting at twelve seconds: 

When the three-pole short circuit to ground occurs in the grid from twelve seconds to twelve point one seconds, zero point five seconds to zero point six seconds new time, the voltage dip is somewhat cushioned by the rise in the excitation voltage.

The generator's torque angle approaches zero because the generator is relieved of active load. You see also that upon relief, the speed begins to rise steadily until the fault is cleared. The unloading thus results in an increase in kinetic energy and consequently in the machine's higher speed.

Short circuit near the generator:

Now the fault is placed directly on the generator bus bar. As shown in figure one, the torque angle now rises to over ninety degrees. The voltage drops even more sharply. The generator speed does not increase, but instead falls steadily. Why is this the case, even though the generator should be unloaded? We will now take a closer look at the fault scenario for a short circuit fault near the generator. The power dissipated at the fault point is higher here at approximately two hundred megawatts than the power supplied.

The fifty-megawatt difference is drawn from the machine's potential energy, causing the machine speed to drop. The load angle approaches ninety degrees. The peak of the short circuit current is now higher than in the case of a short circuit far from the generator. The peak is about eighty kiloamperes.

After the short circuit fault is cleared, it takes some time for the current amplitudes to settle back to the value prior to the occurrence of the short circuit. The current oscillation is accompanied by speed oscillation at two hertz.

Single-phase ground fault near the generator:

We will now examine the ground fault. To do this, the generator's neutral point is grounded. Then the simulation is started. We observe that the peak of the short-circuit current at one ten kiloamps is now even higher than in the three-phase short-circuit.

The transients of the damper winding, the field winding, and the load angle lasted only about one hundred milliseconds in the three-phase short-circuit In the case of the ground short circuit fault, the transients remain high throughout the entire short circuit fault. No stable steady state condition is established.

The system can be translated into a positive omega and a negative minus omega system. The negative system rotates in the opposite direction to the rotor and induces a voltage with double the frequency into the excitation system and the damper system. These fields can now also be divided into two rotating fields, one with plus omega and one with minus omega.

The field with plus omega compensates for the stator field, and the field with minus omega now has three times omega relative to the stator and induces voltages there at triple the frequency. This continues to repeat, but only the fields with two x to four x the frequency are significant. You can see this by zooming in on the excitation currents and the stator currents from six hundred milliseconds to eight hundred milliseconds and applying LTspice's FFT analysis to the excitation current.

Peaks are visible at sixty hertz, hundred and twenty hertz, and two hundred and forty hertz. In the stator current, peaks are visible at sixty hertz and one hundred and eighty hertz. This also occurs on a smaller scale in a synchronous generator with an asymmetrical load. Delivery of active power and reactive power:

We have already seen the generator's active power output. It is specified by changing the torque at the generator's M pin. Of course, the speed controller must be switched off beforehand. In our example, the machine is first ramped up to an active power of one hundred megawatts.

While ramping up the machine to one hundred megawatts, the load angle rises to approximately ten degrees. Before the simulation, the fault duration is set to one hundred seconds and thus deactivated. Reactive power output is achieved by changing the voltage of the exciter motor at the machine's Vair pin from two hundred volts to one hundred volts between eleven and thirteen seconds, and then from one hundred volts to four hundred volts between thirteen and fifteen seconds It is interesting to note that increasing the excitation reduces the load angle.

Therefore, the overexcited machine becomes more stable. Since the voltage supplied by the grid is relatively stable, the twenty kilovolt voltage is little affected by the excitation. This is different in isolated operation. Here, a change in excitation will cause the voltage to change relatively significantly.

Therefore, it is important to activate voltage regulation during isolated operation. To reduce the time delay between a change in the excitation voltage and a change in reactive power, 

Setting generator's excitation resistance RE to one E four:

This clearly demonstrates the relationship between excitation and reactive power without time delay. At U equals two hundred volts, the generator is within the rated range and the reactive power output is zero. When the excitation voltage drops from two hundred volts to one hundred volts, the generator will draw reactive power from the grid. When the excitation voltage rises from two hundred volts to four hundred volts, the generator will supply reactive power to the grid.

Maximum active power output. What happens if you force the generator to supply more and more active power to the grid or to reduce the excitation further and further? At a certain point, theta approximately seventy to ninety degrees, depending on the machine earlier for salient pole machines, the load angle becomes increasingly higher, and at a load angle of one hundred and eighty degrees, the machine's rotor begins to tilt or slip.

The machine is therefore operated up to a load angle of sixty degrees at most. It is therefore important to always keep an eye on the load angle when increasing active power and possibly increase the excitation to lower the load angle again. In isolated operation, this slippage does not occur. In addition to slipping, a severe voltage sag occurs.

Important:  This operating condition should be avoided at all costs.
