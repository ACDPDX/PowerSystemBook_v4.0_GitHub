Peterson coil is an intelligent protection and stabilization element in medium voltage networks and in some cases, also in high voltage networks. It ensures that single pole ground faults can be controlled safely and without immediate shutdown. This makes it an indispensable component of modern power supply systems.

The Peterson coil is an inductive coil that is connected to the neutral point of the transformer. In a three-phase AC network, capacitive currents flow between conductors and earth. If a ground fault occurs, a capacitive fault current flows through the fault location. This can be dangerously high. In the event of a fault, the Peterson coil generates an inductive current that is opposite to the capacitive fault current, and in the ideal case, compensates for it at the fault location.

A small pure cable network with three hundred ampere earth fault current in the fault case is to be simulated. This is three times the capacitive load current of the cable. The full Earth fault current only occurs in the event of a fault. During normal operation, there is a low neutral point voltage at the Peterson, which means that the nominal current of the Peterson does not flow, but a much lower current.

In the event of a fault with a full Earth fault, the full phase voltage is applied to the Peterson, and the nominal current of three hundred ampere flows. Since the YY transformer has no low ohmic return path for flux in the core of the transformer, the flux have to go through tank and oil.

This is not the goal. Therefore, the star point should only be loaded by maximum of ten percent of the transformer nominal current. If the Y/Y transformer has a five leg core, the star point cannot deliver any current if the second star point is isolated. This is because the low reluctance of the outer legs short the magnetic core for same phased fluxes.

If the second star point is also connected, current can flow from one into the other star points, but current should not be exchanged between the two star points.
