Today we will examine the synchronous generator in grid operation and in constant speed operation. We will simulate synchronization and the subsequent delivery of active and reactive power. We will also discuss short circuits near and far from the generator, particularly the single-pole ground fault.

We will also demonstrate what happens when the maximum active power delivered by the machine is exceeded. Now for the circuit. The circuit contains from left to right, a synchronous generator with thirty-six hundred RPM at sixty hertz and a capacity of two hundred and fifty Megavar ampere, a speed governor, a small five megawatts load, an automatic voltage regulator on the thirteen and eight kilovolt side, a YD eleven transformer, a synchronization switch, a load rated at ten megawatts, a switch for the three-phase fault in the grid, a grid resistor and a two hundred and thirty kilovolt voltage source.

The transformer has a delta winding on the secondary side due to the high generator currents and to filter third harmonic currents. The primary side is connected in a star configuration due to the high voltages on the grid side The transformer is set to linear, parameter M equals one, to avoid nonlinear transients after the generator is switched on at full voltage, parameter air equals one.

The rated excitation voltage of the generator is two hundred volts. To maintain the rated voltage constantly at thirteen-point-eight kilovolts, an automatic voltage regulator, AVR, is provided, which is switched on at T equals point one seconds. The AVR has an excitation range from one hundred volts to four hundred volts to cover both overexcitation and underexcitation of the synchronous machine.

The speed controller has a rated speed of thirty-six hundred RPM and is active from the start. At the start of the simulation, the synchronous generator has a speed of three thousand six hundred RPM. If the synchronous generator is synchronized with the grid after one second, the speed controller is switched off immediately thereafter, and the synchronous generator is ramped up from zero megawatts to a hundred and fifty megawatts of active power between two and ten seconds.

The conversion from active power to the torque requirement for the synchronous generator is given by P equals N omega PM equals torque, P equals number of pole pairs. The number of pole pairs is one, making the generator a high-speed turbo generator. To synchronize the generator quickly starting at one second, the phase angle of the voltage source is set to three hundred and thirty degrees, DPHI equal three thirty.

This ensures rapid synchronization for a three-phase transformer with a rotating connection group of YD eleven, eleven, thirty degrees, three hundred and thirty degrees. Additionally, the AVR has already reached the rated voltage of thirteen point eight kilovolts at the terminals. Synchronization and active power output.

No stable steady state condition is established.

The system can be translated into a positive omega and a negative minus omega system. The negative system rotates in the opposite direction to the rotor and induces a voltage with double the frequency into the excitation system and the damper system. These fields can now also be divided into two rotating fields, one with plus omega and one with minus omega.

The field with plus omega compensates for the stator field, and the field with minus omega now has three times omega relative to the stator and induces voltages there at triple the frequency. This continues to repeat, but only the fields with two x to four x the frequency are significant. Delivery of active power and reactive power. We have already seen the generator's active power output. It is specified by changing the torque at the generator's M pin. Of course, the speed controller must be switched off beforehand. In our example, the machine is first ramped up to an active power of one hundred megawatts.

While ramping up the machine to one hundred megawatts, the load angle rises to approximately ten degrees. Before the simulation, the fault duration is set to one hundred seconds and thus deactivated. Reactive power output is achieved by changing the voltage of the exciter motor at the machine's Vair pin from two hundred volts to one hundred volts between eleven and thirteen seconds, and then from one hundred volts to four hundred volts between thirteen and fifteen seconds It is interesting to note that increasing the excitation reduces the load angle.

Therefore, the overexcited machine becomes more stable. Since the voltage supplied by the grid is relatively stable, the twenty kilovolt voltage is little affected by the excitation. This is different in isolated operation. Here, a change in excitation will cause the voltage to change relatively significantly.

Therefore, it is important to activate voltage regulation during isolated operation. To reduce the time delay between a change in the excitation voltage and a change in reactive power, I briefly set the generator's excitation resistance RE to one E four. This clearly demonstrates the relationship between excitation and reactive power without time delay.

At U equals two hundred volts, the generator is within the rated range and the reactive power output is zero. When the excitation voltage drops from two hundred volts to one hundred volts, the generator will draw reactive power from the grid. When the excitation voltage rises from two hundred volts to four hundred volts, the generator will supply reactive power to the grid.

Maximum active power output. What happens if you force the generator to supply more and more active power to the grid or to reduce the excitation further and further? At a certain point, theta approximately seventy to ninety degrees, depending on the machine earlier for salient pole machines, the load angle becomes increasingly higher, and at a load angle of one hundred and eighty degrees, the machine's rotor begins to tilt or slip.

The machine is therefore operated up to a load angle of sixty degrees at most. It is therefore important to always keep an eye on the load angle when increasing active power and possibly increase the excitation to lower the load angle again. In isolated operation, this slippage does not occur. In addition to slipping, a severe voltage sag occurs.
